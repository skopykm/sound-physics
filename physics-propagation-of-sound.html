Longitudinal vs Transverse

Forget the picture of a water wave. Water is only an analogy, and a misleading one. Sound in our atmosphere is a longitudinal wave — period.

The longitudinal one. Air parcels push forward and fall back along the line the sound travels. Nothing in the air goes up and down. The 3D view below the strips shows both — watch the gold particle in each.
Speed1×
  • Transverse: the medium moves across the direction of travel — a rope shaken up and down, a ripple on water.
  • Longitudinal: the medium moves along the direction of travel — bunching up and spreading out. That is sound in air.
  • The wavy line you see drawn everywhere is a graph, not a photograph. Section 4 unrolls the difference.

The Chain Reaction

A speaker cone pushes the air next to it. That air pushes the air next to it, and so on. The push travels. The air stays where it is.

Picture one straight line of air particles. The cone pushes the first one, the first pushes the second, the second pushes the third. Each particle only moves a little way from its home spot and then settles back.

The push is what travels — particle to particle, all the way to your ear. The air itself goes nowhere. Red = squeezed together (compression). Blue = pulled apart (rarefaction).

What you are looking at

Every dot is a parcel of air. The cone on the left is the only thing you can push. When it moves forward it crowds the air in front of it — a compression region (warm). When it pulls back it leaves a gap — a rarefaction region (cool). Each parcel passes the push to its neighbour and then settles back where it started. The gold particle is one parcel you can keep your eye on: watch it swing around its home position while the pattern sweeps past it.

Particles start moving a little before the compression peak reaches them. That is real: the peak is where particles are moving outward fastest, and the rising pressure on its leading edge has already begun nudging them. Ahead of the wave's front, nothing moves at all — send a single pulse to see it.

The graph below the field plots pressure along the dashed sample line. Drag the cursor: bunched particles line up with a pressure peak, spread-out particles with a trough.

Real sound is far too fast to watch — a 250 Hz wave crosses this room in under a hundredth of a second — so the picture runs slowed down. The note in the corner tells you by how much. Particle movement is also exaggerated: real air parcels swing by micrometres.

Three things people get wrong

The air travels to my ear

It doesn't. Watch the gold particle: it only ever moves a little way from home and comes back. What reaches your ear is the disturbance, handed from parcel to parcel.

Louder means faster

Turn the amplitude up. The cone pushes harder, particles swing further — and the speed readout does not move. Loudness is how far parcels swing, not how quickly the push travels.

Sound crosses a vacuum

Slide the medium to vacuum. No parcels, nobody to pass the push along. The cone moves and nothing arrives. Sound needs something to travel through.

Speed of sound in air at room temperature: 343 m/s (1100 ft/s). It depends on the medium — not on how loud or how high the sound is. A thinner (lighter) gas at the same pressure is actually faster; a heavier one is slower. That is what the medium slider models.

Waveform on Paper & Phase

The wavy line is a graph of pressure over time, drawn on paper. Sound itself is never a transverse wave — this is just how we draw it.

On paper only. The line is pressure plotted against time — sound is not a transverse wave.

Why we draw it this way. Bunching is hard to read at a glance, so we translate: pressure above rest goes up, below rest goes down, time runs left to right. A transverse-looking line — but it is a graph of a longitudinal wave, not a picture of one.

The same graph, later in the course. A microphone is a transducer: it converts acoustic energy into electrical energy — a voltage that rises and falls in step with the pressure. That voltage is an analog signal (an analogue of the pressure) and we plot it against time on this exact graph. A loudspeaker is the transducer run backwards. This drawing is how we will look at sound everywhere: in the air, on a cable, in the software.

One cycle is 360° of phase. Start at the midline (0°), peak at 90°, back through the midline at 180°, trough at 270° (also written −180°), and home at 360° — which is 0° again. Above the midline is compression, below is rarefaction.

Everywhere the line sits above the midline. Below the midline the air is spread out — rarefaction. The height of the line is how hard the air is squeezed or stretched, not how far anything moves up.

Loudness

We measure the amplitude of sound in dB, which stands for decibel. When we measure that amplitude in our atmosphere — as acoustic energy — we use dB SPL: Sound Pressure Level.

dB SPL — sound pressure level. The amplitude of a sound wave, measured as pressure and expressed in decibels. It is a ratio scale, not a ruler: 0 dB SPL is the quietest sound young ears can detect, and every +20 dB is ten times the pressure (every +6 dB is double). That is why the scale runs to 130 while pressures run over a million to one.

A useful rule of thumb: about +10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud to us. The amplitude slider back in Section 3 is this idea in miniature — more swing, more pressure, more dB.

Alberta Occupational Health & Safety (OHS Code, Part 16) sets the workplace limit at 85 dBA for 8 hours, and every +3 dB halves the allowed time: 88 dB → 4 h, 91 → 2 h, 94 → 1 h, 97 → 30 min, 100 → 15 min, 103 → 8 min, 106 → 4 min … 115 dB and up → none without protection. Above 85, hearing protection is required. A theatre at show level can sit right on that line.

Frequency & Hertz

One cycle is one compression plus one rarefaction, together — 360°. We count how many cycles happen in one second and measure it in Hz, which stands for Hertz: cycles per second.

Frequencies this low are below hearing (it starts around 20 Hz) — this is a counting exercise. The next section is where you listen, across the whole 20 Hz – 20 kHz range.

1000 Hz = 1 kHz. Kilo just means a thousand — the same prefix as kilometre. Nothing else changes.

The Audio Spectrum

Human hearing runs from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz. In class we split that range into three regions: lows (20–250 Hz), a big middle (250 Hz–5 kHz), and highs (5–20 kHz).

Summing & Cancelling Pt 1

We never hear one frequency on its own. When two waves share the same air they simply add together — and if one is the exact mirror image of the other, they add up to nothing.

in step — the two waves pile up
Phase offset of wave 20°
0°180°360°
Amplitude of wave 2100%
0%100%

Two identical waves in step add to twice the amplitude — 200%, which is +6 dB. At 180° with equal amplitudes, every push from wave 1 meets an equal pull from wave 2: the particles don't move and there is nothing to hear. Lower wave 2 and the cancellation is only partial — what is left is the difference. In real rooms, perfect cancellation is rare: the two sources are never quite identical.

Summing & Cancelling Pt 2

Two different frequencies in the same air add into one combined motion. The low frequency carries the high frequency along on its back.

The particles do both motions at once: a long slow swing with a fast wiggle riding on it. On paper, the fast wave rides along the slow one. Your ear hears two pitches — but the air is only ever in one place at a time.

Tap a speaker to mute it. One speaker alone: no stripes, only the loudspeaker’s own response — but the far side of the room goes quiet. Spacing 0 = both boxes in one spot.

Drag the listener. Down the centre both paths are equal at every frequency: everything sums — the power alley. Off to the side, some frequencies sum and others cancel at the same seat: comb filtering.

Levels are in dB SPL (see Section 5), read at the slider frequency. The response plot is what a measurement microphone would see at the listener: the loudspeaker's own curve, scaled by distance, with the peaks and dips the second box adds.

Wavelength decides where. In air, λ = 343 m/s ÷ frequency: 500 Hz is 2.3 ft (0.69 m), 2 kHz is 6.8 in (0.17 m). A half-wavelength path difference cancels, a whole one sums — so the stripe spacing is fixed by physics before anyone touches a fader. Raise the frequency and watch them tighten.

Filters

A low-pass filter (also called a high-cut) lets the lows through. A high-pass filter (also called a low-cut) lets the highs through. Use both at once and you have a band-pass.

Not a wall. The cutoff isn't a cliff — the curve tapers past it. That slope is what makes a filter sound natural rather than like a phone line switching off.

Distance & The Atmosphere

Far away, sound is quieter — and it has lost its highs. Over long distances the atmosphere acts like a low-pass filter. Distant thunder rumbles. Close thunder cracks.

Reverberation

Indoors, sound reaches you three ways: the direct sound first, then a few early reflections, then a wash of late reflections. RT60 is how long all of that takes to die away.

Everything runs in real time on one clock: dots, playhead and sound. Walk away from the drum and the direct sound drops while the reverb stays about the same — that ratio is how your ears judge distance indoors. Bigger or more absorptive room → longer or shorter RT60, quieter or louder wash.

Reverb is not echo. Reverb is thousands of reflections your brain hears as one event. Echo is a reflection you hear as a second event — Section 14 is all about that line. And we really only experience reverb indoors: reverb slapped on an outdoor scene sounds fake.

Echo

Reverb: the direct sound and its reflections arrive so close together that we hear them as one event. Echo: the reflection arrives late enough that we hear two separate events.
